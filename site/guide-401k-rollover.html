Changing jobs? Your old 401(k) has four options.

Your 401(k) doesn't have to follow you when you leave, but it can. Here's what each choice means, and a short helper to see which ones fit you. Six-minute read.

The four options side by side

OptionSuits you ifWatch for
Leave it in your old planThe plan has low fees and good funds, or you're leaving at 55 or olderPlans can move small balances out on their own, and old accounts are easy to lose track of
Move it to your new employer's planYou want everything in one place, with employer-plan protectionsNot every plan accepts rollovers, and the fund choice may be narrow
Roll it to an IRAYou want more investment choice, or to combine several old accountsCosts vary widely, and you give up the age-55 exception and some creditor protection
Cash it outIt's a real emergency and nothing else worksIncome tax, often a 10% penalty under 59½, and 20% withheld up front

Which options fit you?

Answer four questions. Nothing you choose leaves your browser.

Does your new job offer a 401(k) that accepts rollovers?
Will you be 55 or older in the year you leave this job?
Do you hold your employer's own stock inside the 401(k)?
Do you need some of this money in the next year?

Your options

General federal rules only. Your plan's own rules, state taxes and the rest of your situation matter, so talk to a tax professional before you act.

Mistakes that cost people money

Not asking for a direct rollover

If the old plan sends the money straight to the new account, nothing is withheld. If it sends you a check, 20% is withheld for tax, and you have 60 days to deposit the full amount, including that 20%, or the difference is taxed.

Missing the age-55 exception

Leave a job in or after the year you turn 55 and you can usually take money from that employer's 401(k) without the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. Move it to an IRA and that exception generally no longer applies.

Rolling over company stock without checking

If your 401(k) holds your employer's stock, a tax treatment called net unrealized appreciation may apply. Rolling the stock into an IRA gives it up, so look at this before moving anything.

Forgetting Roth money and loans

Roth 401(k) money should go to a Roth account to keep its tax treatment. An outstanding 401(k) loan can fall due quickly when you leave, and an unpaid balance can become taxable.
