Changing jobs? Your old 401(k) has four options.

When you leave an employer, your 401(k) doesn't have to follow you, but it can. Here's what each choice means, and a short helper to see which ones fit your situation.

The four options at a glance

OptionGood forWatch out for
Leave it in your old planPlans with low fees and good funds; leaving at 55 or olderSmall balances can be moved out by the plan; easy to lose track of
Move it to your new employer's planKeeping everything in one place, with employer-plan protectionsNot every plan accepts rollovers; investment choices may be limited
Roll it to an IRAMore investment choice; combining several old accountsCosts vary widely; you lose the age-55 exception and some creditor protection
Cash it outA true emergency with no other optionIncome tax, often a 10% penalty under age 59½, and 20% withheld up front

Which options fit you?

Answer four questions. Nothing you choose leaves your browser.

1. Does your new job offer a 401(k) that accepts rollovers?
2. Will you be 55 or older in the year you leave this job?
3. Do you hold your employer's own stock inside the 401(k)?
4. Do you need some of this money in the next year?

Your options

Educational only, based on general federal rules. Your plan's own rules, state taxes and your full situation matter. Talk to a tax professional before you act.

Things that trip people up

Ask for a direct rollover

If the old plan sends money straight to the new account, nothing is withheld. If it sends you a check instead, it must withhold 20% for taxes, and you have 60 days to deposit the full amount, including the 20%, to avoid tax on the difference.

The age-55 exception

If you leave a job in or after the year you turn 55, you can usually take money from that employer's 401(k) without the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. Roll it to an IRA and that exception generally no longer applies.

Company stock

If your 401(k) holds your employer's stock, a special tax treatment called net unrealized appreciation may apply. Rolling that stock into an IRA gives it up, so look at this before you move anything.

Roth money and loans

Roth 401(k) money should go to a Roth account to keep its tax treatment. If you have an outstanding 401(k) loan, leaving your job can make it due quickly; an unpaid balance can become taxable.
